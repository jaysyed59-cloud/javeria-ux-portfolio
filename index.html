UX Case Study

Designing a self-test app people can actually trust.

BugSense — an AI-powered UTI self-diagnostic tool. Research, prototyping, and usability testing for a health app that had to feel clear, private, and safe to use without a clinician in the room.

A note on context: this case study comes from independent research carried out as part of my MSc in Digital Health Management, not from a professional agency or in-house design role. I'm sharing it because it's the most complete, honest demonstration of my UX process I can currently offer — research through prototyping through evaluation through iteration — and because I'm looking to build further hands-on experience in exactly this kind of work within a product team.
01
Overview

The problem

BugSense pairs a culture-based test strip for urinary tract infections with a companion app that reads the strip via smartphone camera and delivers results directly to the user. My job was the user-facing app experience — not the diagnostic hardware or the AI model behind it — with one goal: make a clinical, sometimes embarrassing process feel clear and safe enough to complete alone, at home.

My responsibility spanned the full UX process: research, wireframes and prototype, heuristic evaluation, usability testing, and documenting what changed as a result.

02
Research

What users actually needed

Rather than designing from assumptions, I ran a 162-respondent survey (Qualtrics, analyzed in SPSS) with adults in Germany to understand awareness, trust, and expectations around AI-based self-diagnostic tools.

  • Step-by-step guidance through the testing process
  • Plain language, free of medical jargon
  • Large buttons and readable text
  • Audio or voice instructions
  • Easy access to help or technical support

These weren't design preferences I assumed — they came directly from respondents, and they shaped the prototype's structure from the start.

03
Design

Wireframes & prototype

Based on the research, I designed a low-fidelity interactive prototype in Figma covering the core journey: account creation and sign-in, test initiation, step-by-step testing guidance with video support, test history, and the final result screen with a downloadable report and consultation booking.

04
Evaluation

Heuristics before users

Before testing with real people, I checked the prototype myself against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics — visibility of system status, match with the real world, user control, consistency, error prevention, recognition over recall, flexibility, minimalist design, error recovery, and help/documentation. This caught structural issues early: rephrasing clinical terms like "UTI detected" in plain language, and making sure test history was always visible rather than something users had to remember to look for.

05
Validation

Usability testing with real users

I ran moderated usability testing with 22 participants (ages 18–44, evenly split by gender), each completing a structured set of tasks: signing up, reading the privacy policy, working through the user guide, starting a test, following the steps, reviewing a simulated result, and finding support options.

100%rated the app easy to navigate and understand
95.5%felt confident and would use it at home
22participants, ages 18–44

Open-ended feedback surfaced issues a heuristic review alone hadn't caught: a less cluttered profile icon, separating the "Book Consultation" and "Download Report" buttons to avoid accidental taps, and more visual guidance during the actual test step.

06
Outcome

What I'd do differently

The combination of heuristic evaluation and real user testing produced a prototype people navigated confidently and trusted enough to say they'd use for a genuinely private health task. Next time, I'd test with a more diverse group — my sample skewed young and digitally confident — and move from low-fidelity to a higher-fidelity interactive build to test finer details like micro-interactions and error states.

Process summary

  1. Research: 162-respondent survey, analyzed in SPSS, to ground decisions in real priorities.
  2. Design: low-fidelity interactive prototype in Figma covering the full core journey.
  3. Evaluation: self-conducted review against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics.
  4. Validation: moderated usability testing with 22 participants, quantitative + qualitative.
  5. Iteration: concrete design changes identified and documented from real feedback.
